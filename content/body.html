Three brutal truths before we start

Nobody can make a video go viral on command. The people who look like they can are running a loop. They test lots of hooks, keep the winners and drop the losers, and a viral hit is what that loop spits out every now and then. So the skill to build is the loop.

Shorts pay almost nothing. Most channels earn about 3 to 10 cents for every 1,000 Shorts views, and a study of 274 channels found Shorts earn only 3 to 14 percent of what long videos earn per view.

Shorts live on their own island. YouTube split the Shorts recommendation engine away from long videos in late 2025, and the 3.3-billion-view study found long videos turn viewers into subscribers better. Shorts won't quietly grow your documentary channel for you.

So why go all in?

Because Shorts are the cheapest hook lab on the planet. You can test 30 openers a month for almost no money, and every test teaches you something about your audience. Your buyers are also a small, specific crowd, so for fromSilicon, 500 of the right founders and investors watching beats a million random people.

Think of it like this

Shorts are free samples at a supermarket. Nobody gets rich handing out samples. You hand them out so people come back for the full meal (your long videos) or hire you to cater the party (a fromSilicon retainer).

Your Shorts have three jobs

Test hooks fast. Every Short is a cheap experiment. The winners become your long-form titles.
Get seen by the right people. Founders and investors scroll too. Put your name in their feed every day.
Make proof you can sell. Your own test numbers become the case studies that win retainers.

2How the Shorts machine decides

When you post a Short, YouTube doesn't show it to everyone. Picture a restaurant that serves your new dish to one small table first. If that table eats it, the kitchen sends it out to a bigger table, and then a bigger one. If the first table pushes the plate away, the dish goes back to the kitchen and stays there.

Illustration of how Shorts get tested, not real audience sizes.

Every table is a test, and the test watches a few simple numbers. Learn these four and you can read any Short's report card.

Viewed vs swiped awayThe bouncer at the door

The share of people who stayed instead of swiping. In a study of Shorts with 3.3 billion views, Shorts under 60 percent did poorly and Shorts between 70 and 90 percent did better. A high number doesn't promise a hit, but a low one almost always means the hook broke.

Average percentage viewedHow much of the meal they ate

How much of your Short people watched, on average. Loops can push this above 100 percent, because people rewatch part of it.

Engaged viewsThe real views

Since 2025, a Shorts view counts every time a Short starts playing, even during a quick scroll. Engaged views keep the old count of people who stayed past the first few seconds, so use this number when you compare Shorts.

SatisfactionDid they leave happy?

YouTube now rewards what leaves people satisfied, not only what keeps them watching. In January 2026 it was testing dislike and not-interested buttons on Shorts, so bait-and-switch hooks are getting easier to punish.

What broke? A quick doctor's check

Lots of people swiped away?
Fix frame one and your first line. The rest of the Short doesn't matter yet, because almost nobody saw it.
People stayed, then left fast?
Your hook promised something the next few seconds didn't give. Move the proof closer to the start.
People watched it all, but nobody followed?
The Short doesn't point anywhere. Make it part of a series, so one good Short makes people want the next one.

3The hook: the Receipt Hook

After all that data, one hook shape keeps showing up near the top, and it matches the rule in your own long-form rubric. You show the proof first, say something people would bet against, and crack it open before they can swipe. I call it the Receipt Hook, because a receipt is proof that something really happened.

1. Show

Put the receipt on screen at frame one: a number, a headline, a screenshot, a before-and-after, or a timer. No logo, no hello, no face-only opener.

0.0 seconds
2. Say

Say one line a smart person would bet against, in 12 words or fewer. Say it at full speed from the very first syllable.

0 to 2 seconds
3. Crack

By second three, open a crack: a "but", a stake, or a question that the rest of the Short will answer.

The word math

People talk at about 150 to 175 words a minute. That's roughly 2.5 to 3 words a second, so your first three seconds hold about 8 words. Every one of those words has to earn its place.

Dead
Heyguys,welcomebacktothechannel,today
Alive

Same three seconds and the same number of words. One spends them on a hello, and the other spends them on a reason to stay.

Why each part works

  • Show. In the 4,000-video study, "proof drop" openers (a chart, a receipt or a specific number) got the most saves by far, about 1,761 per video. Videos where the creator stood in front of the source material, the greenscreen format, averaged about 150,000 views, compared with about 56,000 for a plain talking head.

  • Say. Bold "hot take" and "investigator" openers averaged about 140,000 views. Openers that start a story slowly, like "So the other day...", averaged about 7,000. That's roughly 20 times worse.

  • Crack. Your rubric says the viewer should never reach a resting place. Your long-form hits change direction with a "but" about every 26 seconds, and a Short has to land its first crack in about 3.

What to actually do

On YouTube Shorts, aim for 35 to 60 seconds for Receipts. On Reels, TikTok and LinkedIn, commentary and clips can run 60 to 90 seconds. Your build-in-public episodes already run 80 to 110 seconds, which suits Reels and TikTok and still fits under YouTube's three-minute Shorts limit. Instagram mostly stops recommending Reels to non-followers once they pass three minutes, so stay under that.

The rule: use the shortest length that lets the payoff land. Extra seconds are something you earn by holding people, and filler never earns them. A joke told too long dies, and a story told too short makes no sense, so let your retention graph tell you which mistake you're making.

6After the hook: the body

A great hook with a boring middle is a trailer for a bad movie. The body has one job: keep paying off the hook, one small surprise at a time. Here is the beat map for a 35-second Short.

0 to 3 seconds: hook
Show, say, crack.
3 to 10 seconds: proof
Zoom into the receipt and add one hard detail, like a date, a name or a number.
10 to 20 seconds: turn
A second surprise. This is your "but" moment.
20 to 32 seconds: payoff
Answer the question you opened with the crack.
Last 2 to 3 seconds: loop
A last line that runs straight back into your first line.

Change something every 3 to 5 seconds: a cut, a zoom, new text or a new fact. Your long-form hits change direction about every 26 seconds, and a Short needs that same heartbeat running about five times faster.

Illustration, not real data. Think of retention as a waterslide: a good one is long and smooth, and people climb back up to go again. A bad hook is a cliff at the top.

The loop trick

Write your last line so it runs straight into your first line. When the Short restarts, the sentence finishes itself, and many people watch the start again before they notice.

End: "So the next time someone tells you a tiny team can't win, remember that..."
Start: "Instagram had 13 employees when Facebook paid $1 billion for it."

A fake loop, where you cut before the payoff, makes people feel tricked, and unhappy viewers are exactly what YouTube is now measuring. A good loop pays off first, then rewards the second watch.

10The all-in Shorts system

Going all in means running three lanes on a fixed weekly rhythm, with a scoreboard. Posting random Shorts every day until one hits is how people burn out in a month.

Lane 1: Receipts

Startup and VC stories with proof on screen. This lane runs on your documentary research, so every long-form script you write should give you five to ten Shorts.

Lane 2: Clips

Moments harvested from client podcasts, with written permission from each client. Every clip that performs becomes a case study you can sell.

Lane 3: Build

autoBlade in public, on your Monday, Wednesday, Friday rhythm. Monday shows something working, Wednesday explains how something works, and Friday admits something.

The biggest jump comes from going from 1 or 2 posts a week up to 3 to 5, and follower growth more than doubled at that level. More posts still helped, just a bit less with each step. On LinkedIn, 2 to 5 posts a week was the sweet spot, worth about 1,182 extra impressions per post compared with posting once a week. X is the odd one out, because a per-creator cap means posting more there spreads your reach thinner.

Your cadence: one Short a day on YouTube, Reels and TikTok, and your best 3 to 5 a week on LinkedIn and X.

The weekly machine

  1. Research on Monday morning. Pick 7 topics and find the receipt for each one before you write a single word.

  2. Hooks first. Write three first lines for every topic, score them with the scorecard below, and keep the best one.

  3. Batch film in one two-hour session. Seven Shorts, one setup, one shirt.

  4. Edit fast. Run clips and build videos through autoBlade, and do Receipts as greenscreen over the source.

  5. Post native. Upload the real file to each platform, with captions burned in and no watermarks.

  6. Score at 48 hours and again at 7 days. Log every hook in your sheet.

  7. Recycle. Winning patterns get new topics, and losing Shorts get a new first line and a second chance on another platform.

13The bigger game: long-form YouTube

Shorts get you seen. Long videos build trust, pay better and turn strangers into people who know your name. Your documentary series is where fromSilicon's reputation gets made, and the long-form machine plays by different rules.

  • YouTube ranks long videos on how satisfied people feel afterward, not only on minutes watched.

  • Test & Compare lets you test up to three titles or thumbnails on one video, and it picks the winner by watch time. Run it on every upload.

  • YouTube put cutting AI slop on its 2026 priority list, so channels that remix the same public facts are getting squeezed. Your edge is access: real investors and founders on camera, which your client work already gives you.

  • Crowded topics need a receipt nobody else has. Sam Altman's story has been told by a lot of channels, so your version needs an interview, a document or a person who was in the room.

  • Use Shorts as your title lab. When a Short's first line wins, try it as a long-form title in Test & Compare.

14X: the dinner party

X works like a dinner party. Nobody listens to the stranger shouting from the doorway, and everyone listens to the guest who has been talking with them all night.

  • X published its Grok-powered feed code on GitHub in January 2026, so a lot of the guesswork is gone.

  • A bookmark counts about ten times as much as a like, and a reply that you answer counts far more than both.

  • There's a cap on how many of your posts one person sees, so posting ten times a day just spreads you thinner.

  • Since a July 2026 update, your posts reach people you follow each other with much more often than strangers.

  • Posts with links get about 30 to 50 percent less reach in most analyses. One reading of the code disputes that, but putting the link in the first reply costs you nothing.

  • The ranker leans heavily toward Premium accounts, so pay for it.

15LinkedIn: the conference hallway

LinkedIn is the hallway at a conference. People are there to learn from someone in their field, and that's exactly where emerging fund managers and founders hang out between meetings.

  • It rewards clear expertise in a niche, and it buries generic AI-written posts.

  • Your connections and followers see your posts first, so who you're connected to matters more than any trick.

  • Buffer's study of over 2 million posts found 2 to 5 posts a week is the sweet spot, worth about 1,182 more impressions per post than posting once a week.

  • Upload your Shorts directly as native video instead of linking out to YouTube, because every platform prefers to keep people on its own site.

Your move on LinkedIn

Post your best Short of the week natively with a two-line text hook. Once a week, write a document post that shows a real before-and-after from client work, with the numbers.

17How to upsell yourself

A chef with no reviews has to charge less, no matter how good the food is. Your channel and your numbers are your reviews.

"Distribution strategist" is a bold title, and your own rubric has a rule for bold claims: no proof object, no license. So go collect the proof.

  • One flagship hit on your own channel. Investors will check it before they read your pitch.

  • Analytics access on every client channel, so you can show before-and-after numbers.

  • Your hook log with 30 or more tests. Almost nobody in VC content has one, and it turns your taste into a system people can pay for.

  • Case studies with real numbers, in a shape like "We changed the first line, and viewed vs swiped went from 52 to 74 percent." That's an example shape, so use your real numbers.

19Words you'll see

Hook
The first one to three seconds of a video. Its only job is to stop the thumb.
Receipt
Proof on screen, like a number, a headline, a screenshot or a timer.
Crack
The "but", stake or question that makes the viewer need the next few seconds.
Viewed vs swiped away
The share of people who stayed instead of swiping to the next Short.
Engaged views
Views from people who stayed past the first few seconds. Since 2025 this is closest to the old meaning of a view.
Average percentage viewed
How much of the video people watched on average. Loops can push it past 100 percent.
Loop
An ending that runs straight back into the opening, so people watch again.
RPM
How much you earn for every 1,000 views after the platform takes its cut.
Greenscreen
You on camera in front of the source, like an article, a chart or a clip.
Trial Reels
Instagram Reels shown to non-followers first, which makes them a free hook test.
Test & Compare
YouTube's tool for testing up to three titles or thumbnails on one video.
Mutuals
People on X who follow you while you follow them back.
Retainer
A monthly fee for ongoing work, instead of a price per job.
CTA
Call to action, like "follow for more". The data says to let the payoff do the asking.
